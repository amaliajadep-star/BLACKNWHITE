01 / BEFORE THE CHANGE

PROOF OF THE
PROBLEM.

01

The Civil Rights Movement grew because racial discrimination was not simply an individual experience. It was built into laws, institutions and everyday life. Historical documents, government records and photographs help demonstrate the inequalities that Black Americans faced. These records show that the movement was responding to real and measurable barriers.

THE CENTRAL ISSUE

INEQUALITY WAS
PART OF EVERYDAY LIFE.

Segregation affected public spaces, education, employment and political participation. The evidence below shows different ways racial inequality was experienced and documented in the United States.

THE PROBLEM WAS
MORE THAN AN OPINION.

Government records, court decisions, photographs and historical accounts provide different forms of evidence. Together, they help establish that racial inequality was present in law, public institutions and everyday opportunities.

WHY THIS EVIDENCE MATTERS.

Without understanding the conditions that existed, it is difficult to understand why people organised protests, challenged laws and demanded change. These records help explain what the movement was fighting against.

02 / AFTER THE CAMPAIGNS

PROOF OF THE
CHANGE.

02

The movement's impact can be seen in changes to laws, government policy and public participation. These achievements did not end racism, but they changed important parts of American society and created new legal protections. The documents below show some of the changes that followed years of campaigning.

THE LEGAL TURNING POINTS

FROM DEMANDS
TO LAW.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965 were major legislative achievements. Their original government records provide direct evidence of the legal changes secured during the movement.

CIVIL RIGHTS ACT

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination in many public places and made employment discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex or national origin illegal. It strengthened the federal government's ability to address discrimination.

THE CHANGE WAS
WRITTEN INTO LAW.

These laws are important evidence because they show that the movement's demands led to formal changes in federal legislation. They also show that the government recognised the need to address racial discrimination and barriers to voting.

EVIDENCE 04 / VOTING RIGHTS AND PUBLIC ACTION
Photograph unavailable. Open the Library of Congress record below.
SELMA TO MONTGOMERY / 1965

THE JOURNEY FOR EQUAL RIGHTS.

Participants in the Selma to Montgomery march demonstrated for voting rights. The 1965 marches brought national attention to racial discrimination in voter registration and helped build pressure for federal voting rights protections.

VIEW LIBRARY OF CONGRESS PHOTO RECORD ↗

WHAT THE VOTING RIGHTS ACT ACTUALLY DID.

The National Archives explains that the 1965 Act outlawed discriminatory voting practices used in many Southern states, including literacy tests. It also provided for federal examiners to register eligible voters in certain jurisdictions.

This is direct evidence of a change in the way voting discrimination could be challenged and addressed by the federal government.

EVIDENCE 05 / WHAT STILL REMAINED
PROGRESS DOES NOT MEAN PERFECTION

THE LAWS CHANGED.
THE STRUGGLE CONTINUED.

Legal victories were significant, but they did not immediately remove every form of racial inequality. Discrimination, economic disadvantage and unequal opportunities continued to affect communities.

ONGOING ISSUE / ECONOMICS

ECONOMIC INEQUALITY

The end of some forms of legal segregation did not automatically remove differences in income, wealth or access to opportunity. Economic inequality remained an important issue after the movement's major legislative achievements.

WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE TELL US?

The evidence shows both achievement and limitation. The movement helped secure major legal protections and challenge official discrimination. However, legislation alone could not remove every social and economic inequality. The movement made substantial progress, but the wider struggle for racial equality continued.

CHANGE CAN BE MEASURED.

The original legislation, historical records and government statistics make it possible to examine what changed and what remained. They show that the Civil Rights Movement had a lasting impact, even though it did not solve every problem it confronted.
