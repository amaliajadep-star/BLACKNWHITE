01 / BEFORE THE CHANGE

PROOF OF THE
PROBLEM.

01

The Civil Rights Movement grew because racial discrimination was not simply an individual experience. It was built into laws, institutions and everyday life. Historical documents, government records and photographs help demonstrate the inequalities that Black Americans faced. These records show that the movement was responding to real and measurable barriers.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / UNDERSTANDING THE PROBLEM

SEGREGATION WAS A SYSTEM, NOT AN ISOLATED INCIDENT.

To understand why the Civil Rights Movement developed, it is necessary to look beyond individual examples of racism and examine the wider system in which they occurred. For much of the twentieth century, racial segregation shaped public life across the United States. In many Southern states, Jim Crow laws and local practices separated Black and white Americans in schools, transport, restaurants and other public facilities. These restrictions were supported by government authorities, while discriminatory customs and unequal access to services extended beyond the wording of individual laws.

Segregation was not simply a matter of people choosing to remain separate. It affected where people could go, which services they could use and how they were treated by public institutions. Black Americans also faced barriers to voting, including literacy tests, registration obstacles and intimidation. These restrictions limited political participation and made it harder for affected communities to challenge the decisions being made about their lives.

This distinction is important when evaluating the movement. If discrimination had only involved individual attitudes, changing personal opinions might have been enough to address it. However, because inequality was reinforced through laws, institutions and established practices, activists also needed to challenge the systems that maintained it. Their demands therefore included legal protection, equal access to public spaces, fair treatment in education and employment, and the ability to participate in democratic processes.

What the historical evidence establishes: racial inequality was embedded in multiple areas of public life. This helps explain why the movement developed into a broad campaign for legal and institutional reform rather than remaining a collection of unrelated protests.

INEQUALITY WAS
PART OF EVERYDAY LIFE.

Segregation affected public spaces, education, employment and political participation. The evidence below shows different ways racial inequality was experienced and documented in the United States. WHY THE MONTGOMERY BUS BOYCOTT MATTERS.

The Montgomery Bus Boycott demonstrates how a local act of resistance could develop into an organised campaign for wider social change. Following Parks' arrest in December 1955, Black residents of Montgomery organised a boycott of the city's buses. The campaign depended on sustained participation: people had to find other ways to travel, coordinate their actions and continue their refusal to use the buses despite the difficulties involved.

Its importance was not limited to the immediate issue of seating on public transport. The boycott challenged the assumption that segregated arrangements would continue without opposition. It also demonstrated the influence that collective action could have when a community coordinated its choices over an extended period. The campaign eventually contributed to a legal challenge to bus segregation, showing how protest and legal action could work alongside one another.

Parks should not be understood as someone who accidentally started a movement through one isolated decision. She was already involved in civil rights activism, and the boycott emerged from existing community organisation and resistance. This is an important distinction because it recognises the work of local activists rather than reducing the history to a single famous individual.

SOURCE ANALYSIS / PHOTOGRAPHS AS HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Photographs can show people, places, public demonstrations and visible responses to events. They are valuable because they preserve a visual record from the period being studied. However, a photograph captures only one moment and one viewpoint. It cannot automatically explain the motives of everyone pictured or represent the experiences of every person involved.

The photographs in this section also reveal different parts of the movement. The image of Parks documents an individual activist facing arrest, while photographs connected to the Brown decision show the importance of legal challenges. Images of demonstrations provide evidence of collective participation and public demands. Together, these sources help show that the movement involved several approaches rather than relying on one leader or one type of action.

Comparing photographs with court records, laws, speeches and eyewitness accounts creates a stronger and more balanced understanding of the movement. A photograph may establish that a particular event or gathering was recorded, but other sources are needed to explain its causes, outcomes and wider significance.

THE MOVEMENT USED MORE THAN ONE FORM OF RESISTANCE.

The historical record also shows that the movement did not depend on a single method of protest. Boycotts challenged businesses and public services through collective refusal. Sit-ins challenged segregation by placing demonstrators directly in spaces where they were denied equal access. Marches and demonstrations made demands visible in public, while Freedom Rides challenged segregation in interstate transport. Legal organisations also used court cases to challenge discriminatory laws and government practices.

These methods were connected. Public protest could attract attention to an issue, while legal action could challenge the rules behind it. Community organisations helped coordinate campaigns, communicate with participants and maintain pressure over time. The use of different methods allowed activists to challenge racial inequality at local, state and national levels.

This is important because it helps explain how the movement was able to reach different audiences. A court case could challenge a government institution directly, while a demonstration could communicate a demand to the wider public. A boycott could place economic pressure on a business or service. The methods were not identical, but they could contribute to the same broader objective of challenging racial discrimination.

EVIDENCE 02 / LAWS AND INSTITUTIONS

Photographs help show what discrimination and resistance looked like, but written records reveal how inequality was supported by institutions. Court decisions, legislation and government documents allow historians to examine the rules that shaped people's lives.

WHY THE LAW WAS A CENTRAL PART OF THE STRUGGLE.

One of the movement's major challenges was that segregation was supported by official rules and established government practices. This meant that activists could not rely only on individual acts of protest to secure lasting change. They also needed to challenge the legal foundations of discrimination and seek protection from the federal government.

Legal action became an important strategy, particularly through organisations such as the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP). Lawyers challenged discriminatory laws through the courts, arguing that segregation violated constitutional protections. The Supreme Court's decision in Brown v. Board of Education in 1954 was a major turning point because it ruled that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional.

However, a legal decision and its implementation are two different things. The Brown ruling did not mean that every segregated school immediately integrated. Resistance from some state and local authorities, along with wider opposition to desegregation, meant that the process was uneven and often contested. This gap between a legal principle and what happened in practice is important when assessing the limits of legal reform.

WHAT BROWN v. BOARD ACTUALLY CHANGED.

Brown v. Board of Education was decided in 1954. The Supreme Court ruled that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional because separate educational facilities were inherently unequal. This decision challenged the legal basis of school segregation and rejected the idea that racial separation could be justified by claiming that facilities were equal.

The decision was significant because it established a constitutional argument against segregation in public education. However, it did not automatically produce equal access to education. In 1955, the Court addressed implementation in Brown II, directing desegregation to proceed with what it described as "all deliberate speed". In practice, resistance and delays meant that integration remained contested.

This difference between a legal ruling and its practical effects is important evidence when evaluating social change. A court can establish that a practice is unconstitutional, but it cannot guarantee that every institution immediately follows the ruling. The continuing resistance to school integration shows why further campaigning and federal intervention remained necessary.

Historical judgement: Brown v. Board was a major legal victory, but it was not the end of school segregation in practice. Its importance lies in changing the constitutional position and giving activists a stronger legal foundation for challenging discrimination.

THE PROBLEM WAS
MORE THAN AN OPINION.

Government records, court decisions, photographs and historical accounts provide different forms of evidence. Together, they help establish that racial inequality was present in law, public institutions and everyday opportunities. This is important because it shows why activists demanded more than changes in individual attitudes: they also challenged the systems that maintained unequal treatment.

WHY THIS EVIDENCE MATTERS.

Without understanding the conditions that existed, it is difficult to understand why people organised protests, challenged laws and demanded change. These records help explain what the movement was fighting against. They also provide a way to check historical claims rather than relying on a single person's interpretation.

ASSESSING THE OUTCOME / FROM CAMPAIGNING TO REFORM

HOW CAN THE IMPACT OF A SOCIAL MOVEMENT BE MEASURED?

The success of a social movement cannot be measured only by the number of demonstrations it organised or the attention it received. It is also necessary to examine whether its demands led to changes in laws, institutions, public policy or people's ability to participate in society. For the Civil Rights Movement, some of the clearest evidence of change can be found in federal legislation passed during the 1960s.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 were important because they addressed different forms of discrimination. The Civil Rights Act introduced protections relating to public accommodations and employment, while the Voting Rights Act targeted discriminatory practices that prevented eligible citizens from registering and voting. These laws did not address every form of inequality, but they changed the legal responsibilities of government institutions and other organisations.

Their passage also demonstrates that the movement had influenced national political decision-making. Campaigners had spent years organising, protesting, challenging discriminatory practices and drawing attention to unequal treatment. The resulting legislation provides evidence that these demands contributed to formal political and legal reform.

How success is evaluated: The strongest evidence of the movement's impact combines legal changes with evidence of their implementation and the experiences of the people affected by them.

THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN LEGAL CHANGE AND SOCIAL CHANGE.

The school photographs demonstrate an important distinction in the history of the movement. A legal decision can change what the law permits or requires, but it does not automatically change people's behaviour, beliefs or willingness to follow that law. The implementation of desegregation depended on decisions made by school authorities, local governments and wider communities.

This meant that legal victories were necessary but not always sufficient. Even after segregation in public schools was declared unconstitutional, resistance continued in different forms. Some communities attempted to delay integration, while students and families could face hostility when entering previously segregated schools.

The evidence therefore suggests that social change occurs through more than one stage. Laws can establish new rights and responsibilities, but their effects depend on implementation, enforcement and continued public participation. This helps explain why the movement continued campaigning after major legal victories had been achieved.

THE CIVIL RIGHTS ACT OF 1964.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was a major federal legislative achievement. It addressed discrimination in several areas of public life, including access to public accommodations and employment. This was significant because it challenged forms of racial exclusion that had been maintained by businesses, institutions and established practices.

The Act also strengthened the federal government's role in addressing discrimination. Rather than leaving every issue to individual states or local authorities, federal law provided stronger grounds for challenging discriminatory practices. This represented an important change in the relationship between civil rights and government responsibility.

However, the Act should not be interpreted as proof that racial inequality ended in 1964. A law can prohibit discrimination without immediately removing the social attitudes, economic disadvantages or institutional barriers that have developed over many years. The Act was a substantial legal victory, but the evidence of continuing inequality shows why further action remained necessary.

THE VOTING RIGHTS ACT OF 1965.

The Voting Rights Act addressed a different but equally important issue: the ability of Black Americans to participate in elections. Although voting rights were protected by constitutional amendments, discriminatory practices continued to prevent many eligible citizens from registering. Literacy tests, unfair registration procedures and intimidation were among the barriers used in some areas.

The 1965 Act gave the federal government stronger powers to address these practices. It prohibited certain discriminatory voting requirements and provided for federal examiners to register eligible voters in jurisdictions covered by the law. This was important because it moved beyond simply declaring that citizens had voting rights and introduced mechanisms intended to make those rights more accessible.

The Act demonstrates that political equality requires more than the formal existence of a right. Citizens must also be able to exercise that right without discriminatory barriers. This is why the Voting Rights Act was such a significant achievement: it addressed obstacles that had prevented people from participating in the democratic process.

They also show that the government recognised the need to address racial discrimination and barriers to voting. Their passage was a major achievement, even though the work of achieving equality continued.

WHY SELMA BECAME A TURNING POINT.

The Selma to Montgomery campaign in 1965 focused national attention on the denial of voting rights. Although Black Americans had formal constitutional protections, discriminatory registration practices meant that many eligible citizens were unable to exercise those rights. The campaign exposed the difference between having a legal entitlement and being able to use it in practice.

Public demonstrations helped bring this issue into wider view. Images and reports of the campaign circulated beyond Alabama, increasing national attention and political pressure. The events showed how local campaigns could influence national discussion when their demands were communicated to a wider audience.

Selma is therefore important not only because of the march itself, but because it demonstrates the relationship between public protest, media attention and government action. The campaign contributed to pressure for stronger federal voting protections. It is an example of how a social movement can use collective action to challenge an institution and encourage political decision-makers to respond.

WHAT THE VOTING RIGHTS ACT ACTUALLY DID.

The National Archives explains that the 1965 Act outlawed discriminatory voting practices used in many Southern states, including literacy tests. It also provided for federal examiners to register eligible voters in certain jurisdictions.

The National Archives records that approximately 250,000 Black Americans registered to vote by the end of 1965. Around one-third of these registrations were completed by federal examiners. This helps demonstrate the role of federal intervention in addressing barriers that local authorities had previously maintained.

However, registration figures do not mean that every eligible citizen could vote without difficulty or that political equality had been fully achieved.

INTERPRETING THE STATISTICS.

Statistics are useful because they can provide measurable evidence of change. The figure becomes more meaningful when it is considered alongside the information about federal examiners. This provides evidence that the Act did more than make a statement about voting rights: it also introduced practical measures intended to improve access.

However, the statistic needs to be interpreted carefully. It measures registrations, not the total number of people who voted, and it does not show that every eligible Black citizen was registered. It also does not measure every form of political influence or the wider experiences of voters. The figure is evidence of an important development, but it is not proof that all voting inequality had disappeared.

What the statistic demonstrates: there was a substantial increase in Black voter registration during 1965, and federal examiners played a measurable role in that change. This supports the argument that the Voting Rights Act produced practical effects, while leaving room to recognise continuing barriers.

EVIDENCE 05 / WHAT STILL REMAINED
PROGRESS DOES NOT MEAN PERFECTION

THE LAWS CHANGED.
THE STRUGGLE CONTINUED.

Legal victories were significant, but they did not immediately remove every form of racial inequality. Discrimination, economic disadvantage and unequal opportunities continued to affect communities. This is why the movement's impact needs to be assessed through both its achievements and its limitations.

WHY THE MOVEMENT'S SUCCESS SHOULD NOT BE OVERSTATED.

It would be inaccurate to describe the Civil Rights Movement as either a complete failure or a complete solution to racial inequality. The movement achieved major changes to federal law and challenged practices that had restricted Black Americans' rights for generations. These achievements changed the legal position of millions of people and provided stronger protections against discrimination.

However, laws do not automatically remove the social and economic conditions that have developed over time. Unequal access to employment, housing, education and economic opportunity could continue even after particular forms of legal segregation were prohibited. Discrimination could also persist through practices that were less visible than formal segregation laws.

This means that the movement's achievements need to be evaluated in relation to both its original goals and the wider conditions that remained. The legal changes were substantial, but they did not create complete equality. Recognising this limitation does not reduce the importance of the movement's achievements; it provides a more accurate assessment of their significance.

THE IMPORTANCE OF A BALANCED CONCLUSION.

When evaluating a social movement, it is important to distinguish between achieving specific reforms and achieving every possible form of social equality. The Civil Rights Movement was successful in securing major legislative changes, expanding legal protections and increasing opportunities for political participation. These outcomes provide clear evidence of its influence.

At the same time, continuing economic and social inequalities demonstrate that legal reform did not resolve every issue. The movement's history therefore shows that social change can be significant without being complete. A movement may achieve important parts of its goals while the wider conditions it challenged remain subjects of further debate and reform.

This is the most balanced way to assess the movement: its achievements should be recognised through the legal and political changes it helped secure, while its limitations should be acknowledged through evidence of the inequalities that continued.

03 / RESEARCH QUALITY

READ THE
RECORD.

03

Historical evidence is not just about finding information. It is also about deciding how reliable, useful and representative that information is. Different sources reveal different parts of the past, which is why this research uses photographs, government documents, legislation and historical collections together.

WHY SOURCE EVALUATION IS NECESSARY.

Historical sources do not all provide the same type of information. A photograph can preserve a visual record of an event, a court decision can establish the legal reasoning behind a ruling, and a government statistic can provide measurable information about a particular issue. Each source has a different purpose and therefore a different set of strengths and limitations.

A source should not automatically be considered reliable simply because it is old, official or preserved by a recognised institution. It is necessary to examine who created it, when it was created, why it was produced and what information it can reasonably establish. A government record may be useful for understanding an official decision, but it may not fully represent the experiences of the people affected by that decision.

Source evaluation also helps prevent conclusions from becoming too broad. For example, one photograph of an integrated classroom can establish that integration was occurring in that particular setting, but it cannot prove that every school was integrated. A registration statistic can demonstrate an increase in registrations, but it cannot explain every individual's experience of voting.

For this reason, the evidence in this project is considered in relation to the specific claims it supports. Rather than treating one source as proof of an entire historical argument, the research compares different forms of evidence to build a more complete explanation.

HOW TO EXAMINE A SOURCE

WHO MADE IT?
WHY DOES IT MATTER?

A source should be considered in context. Its creator, date, purpose and intended audience can affect what information it provides. A photograph may document an event, while a government law records an official decision. Neither source automatically explains the entire historical experience.

01 / ORIGIN

Who created the source, and when? A photograph taken during the movement is a primary visual source. A modern museum article explaining that photograph is a secondary source. Knowing the difference helps identify how close the source is to the event.

02 / PURPOSE

Why was the source created? A government law records official legislation. A newspaper photograph may have been created to report an event to the public. The original purpose can influence what was included and what was left out.

03 / LIMITATIONS

What does the source leave out? One photograph cannot represent every protester, and one law cannot show how every community experienced its enforcement. A source may be valuable while still providing only a partial view.

04 / CORROBORATION

Can another independent source support the same claim? Comparing photographs with legislation, court records and statistics makes an argument stronger because it does not depend on one document or interpretation.

RELIABILITY AND USEFULNESS ARE NOT THE SAME THING.

A source can be reliable for one purpose but limited for another. For example, the original text of the Civil Rights Act is a reliable record of what the federal government legislated in 1964. However, the Act cannot independently show how every community experienced the law or whether all discrimination stopped after it was introduced.

Similarly, a photograph preserved by the Library of Congress can provide a reliable record of the scene it depicts. Its value depends on the information attached to it, including the date, photographer and description. Even when the photograph itself is authentic, it may only represent one moment and cannot explain everything surrounding the event.

This means that reliability should be considered in relation to the question being investigated. A source is useful when it provides information relevant to the research question and when its limitations are recognised. The strongest historical arguments use sources for the claims they can actually support rather than expecting one document to explain an entire period.

WHY CORROBORATION STRENGTHENS THE RESEARCH.

Corroboration involves comparing information from different sources to see whether they support the same conclusion. For example, a photograph of the Selma to Montgomery march can be compared with government records about the Voting Rights Act and historical accounts of the campaign. Each source contributes a different kind of information: the photograph records public participation, the legislation records the government's response, and historical accounts explain the events connecting them.

Comparing sources can also reveal differences between official decisions and people's lived experiences. A law may establish a new right, while photographs, reports and other records show the difficulties involved in making that right meaningful. This comparison prevents the research from presenting legal change as though it automatically produced complete social equality.

PRIMARY AND SECONDARY SOURCES ARE BOTH USEFUL.

A primary source comes directly from the period being studied or from someone who experienced it. Examples include original laws, photographs, court documents and contemporary records.

A secondary source is created later and interprets or explains historical events. Museum exhibitions, history books and educational articles can provide useful context, but they should not be mistaken for the original document or photograph they discuss.

Using both types allows the research to examine original evidence while also understanding its wider historical meaning.

HOW THE SOURCES IN THIS PROJECT WORK TOGETHER.

The photographs from the Library of Congress provide visual evidence of people, places and events connected to the movement. They help make historical events more concrete, while their catalogue records provide information about dates, photographers and context. These photographs are primary sources, although the descriptions and historical explanations accompanying them may have been written later.

The original Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act are primary legal sources. They are particularly useful for identifying the changes made by the federal government because they record the actual legislation. National Archives explanations and Library of Congress exhibitions are secondary material that help place those documents in historical context.

Government statistics add another form of evidence by allowing changes in political participation to be measured. However, statistics need to be read alongside their definitions and time periods. They can demonstrate patterns and changes, but they cannot independently explain every cause behind those patterns.

By combining these sources, the project can examine the movement from several perspectives: the conditions people experienced, the actions they took, the legal changes that followed and the inequalities that continued. This makes the overall argument more balanced than relying on photographs, statistics or legislation alone.

DON'T JUST COLLECT SOURCES. QUESTION THEM.

Strong historical research does not treat every source as complete or unquestionable. It considers what the evidence reveals, recognises what it cannot establish and compares it with other records. This makes the conclusions more balanced and better supported. The purpose of evidence is not simply to decorate an argument: it is to provide a reason for believing that the argument is accurate.
