01 / WHERE IT ALL BEGAN

HISTORY &
EMERGENCE.

01

The American Civil Rights Movement of the 1950s and 1960s was a nationwide struggle led largely by African Americans and their supporters to challenge racial discrimination and segregation. Its emergence was shaped by a much longer history of unequal treatment, organised resistance and demands for equal rights. The movement was not one event or one person — it was the result of generations of people pushing for change.

THE PROBLEM WAS
BUILT INTO THE SYSTEM.

After slavery ended, racial inequality continued through discriminatory laws and practices. In many Southern states, Jim Crow laws enforced racial segregation in public life. Black Americans faced barriers in education, transport, employment, housing and voting.

A TIMELINE OF RESISTANCE
1896

Plessy v. Ferguson

The US Supreme Court upheld racial segregation under the principle of "separate but equal". In practice, facilities and opportunities for Black Americans were often unequal. This decision helped provide legal support for segregation for decades.

1909

The NAACP is founded

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) was established to challenge racial injustice. It became an important organisation in the legal fight against segregation and discrimination.

17 MAY 1954

Brown v. Board of Education

The Supreme Court ruled that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional. The decision challenged the "separate but equal" approach in education, although many communities resisted putting desegregation into practice.

1 DECEMBER 1955

Rosa Parks and the Montgomery Bus Boycott

Rosa Parks was arrested after refusing to give up her seat to a white passenger on a Montgomery bus. Her arrest helped spark a 13-month boycott organised by Black residents. The campaign challenged bus segregation and brought national attention to the movement.

1957

Little Rock and the SCLC

Nine Black students, known as the Little Rock Nine, faced resistance when they attempted to attend Central High School in Arkansas. The Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) was also formed in 1957, helping coordinate civil rights campaigns.

1960

The Greensboro sit-ins

Four Black college students began a sit-in at a segregated lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina. Their action inspired similar demonstrations in other places and encouraged student participation in the movement.

28 AUGUST 1963

The March on Washington

Hundreds of thousands of people gathered in Washington, D.C., to demand jobs, freedom and civil rights. Martin Luther King Jr. delivered his famous "I Have a Dream" speech during the event.

1964

The Civil Rights Act

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibited discrimination in many public places and made employment discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex or national origin illegal. It was a major legal achievement of the movement.

1965

The Voting Rights Act

Following campaigns for voting rights, including the Selma-to-Montgomery marches, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 aimed to overcome barriers that prevented Black Americans from exercising their right to vote.

PEOPLE WHO HELPED SHAPE THE MOVEMENT
ACTIVIST / ORGANISER

ROSA PARKS

Rosa Parks was an experienced civil rights activist whose arrest in 1955 helped trigger the Montgomery Bus Boycott. Her action became an important symbol of resistance to segregation.

MINISTER / LEADER

MARTIN LUTHER KING JR.

King became a prominent leader of the movement and advocated nonviolent protest. He helped lead campaigns that challenged segregation and demanded equal rights.

LAWYER / LEGAL STRATEGY

THURGOOD MARSHALL

As a leading NAACP lawyer, Marshall argued Brown v. Board of Education before the Supreme Court. His legal work helped challenge segregation through the courts.

ACTIVIST / ORGANISER

ELLA BAKER

Baker supported grassroots organising and encouraged young people to take leadership roles. She helped create the conditions for student-led activism to grow.

THEORY CONNECTION: RELATIVE DEPRIVATION

Relative Deprivation Theory helps explain why people may join a social movement when they believe they are being unfairly denied rights or opportunities compared with others. Black Americans experienced unequal access to education, public services, employment and political participation. The contrast between the promise of equal citizenship and the reality of segregation contributed to frustration and demands for change.

This theory helps explain one reason people mobilised, but it does not explain everything. Organisation, leadership, community networks and political opportunities also helped the movement grow.

THE BIGGER PICTURE.

The movement emerged from a long history of inequality, but it was shaped by people who organised, challenged discrimination and refused to accept segregation as something that could never change.

02 / CHALLENGING THE RULES

DEVIANCE &
SOCIAL NORMS.

02

Social norms are the unwritten expectations and rules that influence how people behave in society. During the Civil Rights Movement, segregation was treated as normal in many communities because it was supported by laws, institutions and everyday practices. Activists challenged these expectations by demanding equal treatment.

LOOKING AT DEVIANCE

WHO GETS TO DECIDE
WHAT IS "NORMAL"?

In sociology, deviance refers to behaviour that goes against the norms or expectations of a group or society. It does not automatically mean that the behaviour is harmful or wrong. During the Civil Rights Movement, peaceful protest and civil disobedience challenged rules that activists believed were unjust.

HOW SOCIAL NORMS WERE CHALLENGED
EXAMPLE 01 / PUBLIC TRANSPORT

BUS BOYCOTTS

The Montgomery Bus Boycott challenged the expectation that Black passengers should accept segregated seating. By refusing to use the bus system, participants showed that collective action could challenge an unfair practice.

EXAMPLE 02 / PUBLIC SPACES

SIT-INS

Student activists sat at lunch counters that refused to serve Black customers. Their peaceful presence challenged the social expectation that public spaces could be separated by race.

EXAMPLE 03 / EDUCATION

SCHOOL INTEGRATION

Black students and their families challenged segregated education by demanding access to schools that had previously excluded them. Their actions questioned the idea that separate schools could provide equal opportunities.

EXAMPLE 04 / POLITICAL RIGHTS

VOTING RIGHTS CAMPAIGNS

Activists organised voter-registration campaigns and demonstrations to challenge barriers that prevented Black citizens from participating equally in elections.

BREAKING AN UNJUST
SOCIAL EXPECTATION.

Many activists used nonviolent civil disobedience: deliberately refusing to follow rules or practices they considered unjust. Their actions drew attention to the difference between what the law allowed and what they believed equality should mean.

WHY WAS THIS SEEN AS DEVIANT?

In communities where segregation was accepted or enforced, activists who challenged it could be labelled troublemakers or rule-breakers. Their behaviour went against the dominant social expectations of the time.

However, the label of deviance depends on the social rules being used to judge behaviour. The movement challenged the idea that an established norm was automatically fair or morally right. Over time, activism helped shift public attitudes about segregation and equal rights.

QUESTION THE "NORMAL".

Something being accepted by society does not necessarily make it fair. The Civil Rights Movement shows how people can challenge harmful social norms and help change what society considers acceptable.

03 / HOW A MOVEMENT DEVELOPS

THE MOVEMENT
CYCLE.

03

Social movements often develop through different stages. These stages help explain how an issue gains attention, how people organise around it and how a movement can influence society. The Civil Rights Movement did not follow a perfectly neat sequence, but its development can be understood through a movement-cycle model.

FROM SHARED
FRUSTRATION TO ACTION.

A social movement can begin when people recognise a shared problem. As awareness grows, people may organise, build networks, attract supporters and push institutions to respond.

01

EMERGENCE

People begin recognising a shared issue and expressing concern about it. During the Civil Rights Movement, generations of racial discrimination and segregation created demands for change. Legal challenges and local activism helped build momentum before the 1950s.

02

COALESCENCE

Individuals and groups begin organising around a common goal. Campaigns such as the Montgomery Bus Boycott, student sit-ins and mass demonstrations brought people together and helped the movement gain national attention.

03

INSTITUTIONALISATION

Some movement activities become more organised through established groups, leadership structures and ongoing campaigns. Organisations such as the NAACP and SCLC helped coordinate legal action, community organising and public demonstrations.

04

DECLINE OR TRANSFORMATION

A movement may lose momentum, achieve some goals or transform into new campaigns. Major legal changes, including the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965, marked important achievements. However, the struggle for racial equality continued beyond these laws.

WHY DOES THE CYCLE MATTER?

The movement cycle shows that social change usually requires more than one protest or one influential person. Awareness, organisation, leadership, public participation and pressure on institutions can all contribute to a movement's development.

It also shows that achieving legal change does not mean every social problem has disappeared. Movements can continue, adapt or inspire later campaigns when inequality remains.

CHANGE IS NOT A FINISH LINE.

The Civil Rights Movement achieved major changes in law and helped shift public attitudes. But the movement's history also shows that progress can be uneven and that legal victories do not automatically remove every form of inequality.
